    Multi Model Hackathon · SF Tech Week · Field briefing

    A search drone that looks for one missing person's things, with no internet.

    “The camper had a blue backpack, an orange tent, and a gray dog.”

    You said that into Patrol's console at 14:33. Patrol turns words like these into a search. It sweeps the ground from 40 metres up, drops down for a closer look at anything that might match, and sends back a position, a photo and a short note when it finds something. Four AI models share the work on one laptop, and none of them calls the internet.

    01 · The problem

    Searchers start with a description, not a photo

    When someone goes missing in the backcountry, the first useful facts usually come from family: what the person was wearing, what they carried, whether the dog was with them. Nobody has trained a detector on this person's blue backpack, so any computer that helps has to work from words. Out there it usually can't send video anywhere either, because there is no cell signal.

    Patrol is a prototype of a drone that works from those words by itself. It flies the search, takes a closer look at whatever might match, and only asks for a person's attention when it has something worth checking: a ground position, a photo and one or two sentences.

    Why decide in the air, rather than review the photos after landing

    The closer look

    From 40 m a backpack is a few pixels. In the air, the drone can drop down and settle a “maybe” while it is right there. After landing, every “maybe” costs another flight.

    Time

    A find during the flight beats a find an hour later at a laptop. Cold, injury and avalanches don't wait for a review.

    Bandwidth

    With no cell service, live video can't reach the base. A short alert with a position and a small photo fits a radio link.

    What it is, and what it isn't

    Patrol is decision support. A person reviews every alert. It would sit beside the tools search teams already use (thermal cameras and person detectors for spotting people, avalanche transceivers, RECCO and phone-finding for buried victims), not in place of them. Everything you see here happens in a simulator, which proves how the system behaves, not how well it would detect things on a real hillside.

    03 · Who does what

    Four models fly the search. Three more built the world it searches.

    Each model does the one job it is good at, at the speed that job needs. Plain code ties them together and keeps the drone safe.

    During a flight

    The fast eyes · system one

    Clef-Flash

    A decision model from Cloudflare. You give it a picture and a few typed questions, and it returns a probability for every answer in one pass, without writing any text. Patrol asks it whether each odd-looking spot is one of the clues, whether a close-up confirms it, and whether something you said was a description or a command.

    Size
    9B, built on Qwen3.5-9B, 8-bit MLX
    Speed
    about 0.3 s per spot, 0.4 s per close-up
    Calls
    171 in your flight
    Licence
    Apache-2.0
    The slow thinker · system two

    qwen38

    Qwen3.8 Flash Next, a large language model that can also see images. It turns your description into what each thing looks like from above, writes the short radio note for each find, suggests where to search next, and gives a second opinion when Clef-Flash is unsure. It never sits in the control loop.

    Size
    about 70 GiB of 4-bit weights, served by ds4
    Speed
    2 to 5 s per job
    Calls
    6 in your flight (3 notes, 3 plans)
    The ears

    Parakeet

    NVIDIA's speech recogniser. It turns what you say into text, both the description before take-off and commands during the flight.

    Size
    0.6B (TDT v3)
    Speed
    20 to 90 ms per command
    Licence
    CC-BY-4.0
    The voice

    Kokoro

    A small text-to-speech model. It reads the clue list back to you, acknowledges commands and reads every alert aloud, so your eyes can stay on the drone.

    Size
    82M, voice am_michael
    Speed
    60 to 250 ms per sentence
    Licence
    Apache-2.0
    The spotter · not a trained model

    Anomaly finder

    Classical computer vision on the CPU. It looks for colours that are rare in each photo and boxes the three oddest spots. That is what lets Clef-Flash look at three small crops instead of a whole 1280×960 frame, and it is the reason the drone can fly high.

    Runs on
    CPU, numpy
    Sees
    RGB pixels only
    The pilot

    Code

    The planner lays out lanes and sectors. The gate decides whether a model's answer is strong enough to act on. Safety owns the height limits, the boundary, the battery reserve and the trip home. Models advise. Code flies, and no model can override it.

    Lives in
    patrol/search.py, planner.py, control.py

    Before the flight: the world and its props

    The world

    MuJoCo

    A physics simulator standing in for the real hillside, the drone and its cameras. It renders each camera view in about 10 ms and keeps the answer key (where everything was planted), which is used only to score a flight afterwards.

    Version
    3.15, Apache-2.0
    The prop department

    Z-Image Turbo, Apple Vision, Hunyuan3D

    The backpack, tent, dog and decoys were each made from one sentence by the Mac's 3D rig, m3d. Z-Image Turbo paints a concept picture, Apple Vision cuts the object out of the background, and Hunyuan3D builds a textured 3D model, which is scaled to its real size and dropped into the scene.

    Time
    about 3 minutes of GPU per item, 8 items
    Licences
    Apache-2.0; Tencent Hunyuan community licence

    04 · How the models fit together

    A fast loop that never stops, a slow thinker beside it, and code holding the controls

    Clef-Flash makes every per-photo call because it answers in a third of a second. qwen38 does the few jobs that need real thought, in the background. Voice is how you talk to the drone, and code turns every answer into motion only after checking it.

    Each model sees only what it must

    The finder sees every photo. Clef-Flash sees only three small crops per photo and the close-ups. qwen38 sees only the finds. In your flight that was 375 odd spots, 171 Clef-Flash looks and 6 qwen38 jobs.

    Every answer goes through code

    Clef-Flash's probabilities pass a gate with thresholds. qwen38's plans are kept only if they name real sectors that haven't been searched. A spoken command runs only if Clef-Flash is at least 45% sure what it was.

    When a model fails, the flight goes on

    If Clef-Flash is down or late, the spot stays unjudged, the console says FALLBACK, and the sweep carries on and comes home. Nothing is ever alerted on a fallback answer.

    06 · Fly high, look close

    Sweep from as high as the finder still works, then come down only where it matters

    Height is the battery lever. The higher the drone sweeps, the more ground each photo covers, the fewer lanes it flies, and the more area one battery searches. The catch is that small things turn into a few pixels. Patrol handles that by splitting the job: from up high, it only has to notice that a spot looks odd. Deciding what the spot is happens later, close up.

    The smallest clue sets the height

    Each clue card carries a rough real size, which qwen38 writes along with the description. The planner sweeps at the highest height that works for the smallest thing on the list: 40 m for a 0.55 m backpack, and 21.8 m once a 0.3 m helmet is on the list. That second case exposed a bug today. Photos were still spaced for 40 m, which left gaps at the lower height. The fix landed at 14:53 and now scales the spacing with the height (12 m between photos at 40 m, 6.5 m at 21.8 m).

    Come down only as far as needed

    On a “maybe” the drone flies toward the spot, stops short of it at 20 m and takes an angled zoom close-up, framed to about four metres of ground (or three times the clue's size). If Clef-Flash is clearly yes or clearly no, that settles it. Only an unsure answer sends it down to 12 m for a second look. In your flight all eight closer looks were settled at 20 m, in 8 seconds each on average.
